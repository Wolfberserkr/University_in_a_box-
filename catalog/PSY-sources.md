## Tag key — read this before using the list

| Tag | Means | Trust |
|---|---|---|
| `[V]` | **Verified.** Citation checked against an authoritative record (PubMed, DOI resolver, publisher page) during the session that produced this list. | Cite it. |
| `[R]` | **Recalled.** Plausible from memory. Title, author, year, or existence may be wrong. | Lead to check, not a shopping list. |
| `[H]` | **In hand.** You hold it and it's in NotebookLM. | Strongest. Supersedes `[V]`. Update the tag yourself as items arrive. |

**9 items below are `[V]`. Everything else is `[R]`.** That ratio is the honest state of an LLM used as a librarian, and it is why this agent must run with search enabled. A model produces fluent, correctly formatted citations for work that does not exist — the failure isn't rare, it's the default mode when recall is thin.

The `[V]` items cluster in Term 0 deliberately. Term 0 is the term where a fabricated citation would do the most damage, because it's the term that teaches you to detect fabrication.

**Order of operations:** verify → acquire → upload to NotebookLM → retag `[H]` → then read. Never read from an untagged item.

## TERM 0 — Calibration (Weeks 1–4)

**Not optional and not shortenable.** In the 2015 reproducibility project, 97% of the original studies reported statistically significant results; 36% of the replications did. NotebookLM only knows what you upload — skipping this term means building a very confident engine for repeating debunked findings.

### Primary papers

1. `[V]` Open Science Collaboration (2015). *Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science.* **Science**, 349(6251), aac4716. — PMID 26315443. **The single most important document in the year.**
2. `[V]` Simmons, J. P., Nelson, L. D., & Simonsohn, U. (2011). *False-Positive Psychology: Undisclosed Flexibility in Data Collection and Analysis Allows Presenting Anything as Significant.* **Psychological Science**, 22(11), 1359–1366. doi:10.1177/0956797611417632 — how a true-seeming result gets manufactured without anyone lying.
3. `[V]` Nosek, B. A., Hardwicke, T. E., Moshontz, H., et al. (2022). *Replicability, Robustness, and Reproducibility in Psychological Science.* **Annual Review of Psychology**, 73, 719–748. doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-020821-114157 — the state of play a decade on. Read third, after you know what broke.
4. `[V]` Klein, R. A., Vianello, M., Hasselman, F., et al. (2018). *Many Labs 2: Investigating Variation in Replicability Across Samples and Settings.* **Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science**, 1(4), 443–490. doi:10.1177/2515245918810225 — 28 findings, 125 samples, 36 countries. Kills the "it failed because the sample was different" defense.
5. `[V]` Henrich, J., Heine, S. J., & Norenzayan, A. (2010). *The weirdest people in the world?* **Behavioral and Brain Sciences**, 33(2–3), 61–83. — PMID 20550733. Most of psychology describes an unusual population and was generalized to the species. **Reframes everything on either side of it.**

### The debunked shelf — Week 4 builds this

Studied as case material, never cited as evidence. Each is a famous finding whose autopsy teaches more than the finding did.

6. `[V]` Doyen, S., Klein, O., Pichon, C.-L., & Cleeremans, A. (2012). *Behavioral Priming: It's All in the Mind, but Whose Mind?* **PLoS ONE**, 7(1), e29081. — the elderly-priming failure, plus the experimenter-expectancy demonstration that explains the original.
7. `[V]` Le Texier, T. (2019). *Debunking the Stanford Prison Experiment.* **American Psychologist**, 74(7), 823–839. doi:10.1037/amp0000401 — from the archives, not a re-analysis. Guards were coached.
8. `[V]` Hagger, M. S., Chatzisarantis, N. L. D., et al. (2016). *A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect.* **Perspectives on Psychological Science**, 11(4), 546–573. doi:10.1177/1745691616652873 — willpower-as-a-fuel-tank, 23 labs, no effect. Read Baumeister & Vohs's reply in the same issue (11(4), 574–575) for how a field argues.
9. `[V]` Watts, T. W., Duncan, G. J., & Quan, H. (2018). *Revisiting the Marshmallow Test: A Conceptual Replication.* **Psychological Science**, 29(7), 1159–1177. doi:10.1177/0956797618761661 — the effect is real, roughly half the size, and mostly family background. **The most instructive item on the shelf: not "false," but "much smaller and about something else."**
10. `[R]` Ranehill, E., Dreber, A., Johannesson, M., et al. (2015). *Assessing the robustness of power posing.* **Psychological Science**. Then Carney's own public withdrawal of support for the finding — a rare and worth-reading document.
11. `[R]` Rosenhan, D. L. (1973). *On being sane in insane places.* **Science**, 179(4070), 250–258. Pair with Cahalan's *The Great Pretender* (2019), which alleges the data were partly fabricated. A famous study **and** its own debunking, one week.

### Methods, for reading rather than doing

12. `[R]` Ritchie, S. *Science Fictions* (2020) — the crisis for a general reader, by someone inside it. Best single entry point.
13. `[R]` Cumming, G. *Understanding The New Statistics* (2012) — effect sizes and intervals. Read chapters 1–3 only; the cut list forbids the rest.
14. `[R]` Meehl, P. E. (1990). *Why summaries of research on psychological theories are often uninterpretable.* **Psychological Reports**. Written before the crisis, diagnosing it exactly.

## TERM 1 — The Evolved Mind (Weeks 5–14)

15. `[R]` Sapolsky, R. *Behave: The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst* (2017) — **the spine of the term.** Organized by time scale: one second before the act, then minutes, days, years, millennia. That structure *is* the Term 1 output prompt.
16. `[R]` Henrich, J. *The Secret of Our Success* (2015) — culture as the human adaptation rather than a coat of paint on one. **The highest-value pair on this list with #17.**
17. `[R]` Henrich, J. *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020) — the book-length WEIRD argument, extended to how one culture became statistically strange.
18. `[R]` Boyd, R. & Richerson, P. J. *Not by Genes Alone* (2005) — the formal cultural-evolution model under Henrich's popular treatment.
19. `[R]` Tooby, J. & Cosmides, L. *The Psychological Foundations of Culture* (1992), in *The Adapted Mind* — the founding evolutionary-psychology statement. Read it before reading the objections.
20. `[R]` Buller, D. J. *Adapting Minds* (2005) — the serious critique of evolutionary psychology. **Read #19 and #20 in the same week or don't read either.**
21. `[R]` Trivers, R. (1971). *The evolution of reciprocal altruism.* **Quarterly Review of Biology**, 46(1), 35–57.
22. `[R]` Hamilton, W. D. (1964). *The genetical evolution of social behaviour, I & II.* **Journal of Theoretical Biology**, 7(1), 1–52.
23. `[R]` Nesse, R. *Good Reasons for Bad Feelings* (2019) — why the unpleasant emotions are functional. Directly useful for the capstone.
24. `[R]` Dunbar, R. *Grooming, Gossip and the Evolution of Language* (1996) — treat the specific number with Term 0 skepticism; the argument survives it.

## TERM 2 — Judgment (Weeks 15–24)

**The term the capstone is built out of.** Read it slowest.

25. `[R]` Kahneman, D. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) — read it **after** Term 0, not before. Chapter 4 relies on priming work that didn't hold; Kahneman acknowledged this publicly in 2017. The book remains essential and is a live exercise in reading a great book critically.
26. `[R]` Kahneman, D., Sibony, O., & Sunstein, C. *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (2021) — **the closest thing on this list to your capstone's method.** Variance between judges, and within one judge across time, in exactly the kind of repeated professional decision you'll be auditing.
27. `[R]` Gigerenzer, G. & Todd, P. *Simple Heuristics That Make Us Smart* (1999) — the ecological-rationality counterargument. Heuristics as adapted, not broken.
28. `[R]` Gigerenzer, G. (1996). *On narrow norms and vague heuristics: A reply to Kahneman and Tversky.* **Psychological Review**, 103(3), 592–596. The dispute in its compressed form.
29. `[R]` Meehl, P. E. *Clinical Versus Statistical Prediction* (1954) — **the highest-value single item for your destination.** Seventy years old and still winning: simple actuarial rules beat expert judgment across a startling range of tasks. Week 20.
30. `[R]` Dawes, R. M., Faust, D., & Meehl, P. E. (1989). *Clinical versus actuarial judgment.* **Science**, 243(4899), 1668–1674. The update, and the shorter read.
31. `[R]` Tetlock, P. *Expert Political Judgment* (2005) — experts, scored over 20 years. Chastening.
32. `[R]` Tetlock, P. & Gardner, D. *Superforecasting* (2015) — the constructive sequel. Scoring rules and calibration, which the capstone needs operationally.
33. `[R]` Loftus, E. F. & Palmer, J. C. (1974). *Reconstruction of automobile destruction.* **Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior**, 13(5), 585–589. Memory as reconstruction — directly relevant to statement-based evidence.
34. `[R]` Wells, G. L. & Loftus, E. F., on eyewitness identification — pick a recent review rather than the primary studies; this literature has moved.
35. `[R]` Bond, C. F. & DePaulo, B. M. (2006). *Accuracy of deception judgments.* **Personality and Social Psychology Review**, 10(3), 214–234. **Meta-analysis: humans detect deception at roughly 54%, and training raises confidence far more than accuracy.** Required reading given your occupation; read it as a professional hazard, not trivia.
36. `[R]` Simons, D. & Chabris, C. (1999). *Gorillas in our midst.* **Perception**, 28(9), 1059–1074. Inattentional blindness — one of the classic findings that has held up robustly. Note that it's on the *surviving* side; Term 0 shouldn't leave you thinking nothing survived.

## TERM 3 — The Social Animal (Weeks 25–34)

37. `[R]` Ross, L. & Nisbett, R. *The Person and the Situation* (1991) — **the spine of the term.** The situationist case, argued well, before the crisis softened parts of it.
38. `[R]` Haidt, J. *The Righteous Mind* (2012) — intuition-first moral psychology. Note that Moral Foundations Theory is contested and some supporting work is shaky; read it as a strong argument, not a settled result.
39. `[R]` Greene, J. *Moral Tribes* (2013) — the dual-process account of moral judgment, with the trolley-problem neuroimaging. Hold the imaging results loosely.
40. `[R]` Milgram, S. *Obedience to Authority* (1974) — **only alongside** Perry's *Behind the Shock Machine* (2013) and Gibson's archival work on how variable the actual procedure was. The finding is real; the tidy version isn't.
41. `[R]` Asch, S. E. (1956) conformity studies — read the effect size, not the anecdote. Roughly a third of critical trials, not "everyone conforms."
42. `[R]` Cialdini, R. *Influence* (rev. ed.) — treat as a hypothesis catalogue, not evidence. Several signature studies have replication problems. **Useful precisely as a Term 0 exercise: go through it flagging what you'd need to check.**
43. `[R]` Tajfel, H. & Turner, J. C. (1979). *An integrative theory of intergroup conflict* — social identity theory. Minimal-group findings replicate well.
44. `[R]` Janis, I. *Groupthink* (1982) — historically influential, methodologically weak (post-hoc case studies). Read for the vocabulary and the caution both.
45. `[R]` Staw, B. M. (1976), on escalation of commitment — why organizations pour resources into failing decisions. Relevant to any incident that developed over hours.
46. `[R]` Goffman, E. *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959) — sociology, not psychology, and the best thing on this list about being watched. Given your destination, arguably the most directly applicable book here.
47. `[R]` Fiske, S. T. & Taylor, S. E. *Social Cognition* — a current edition, as the reference text you consult rather than read straight through.

## TERM 4 — The Individual (Weeks 35–44)

48. `[R]` John, O. P., Robins, R. W., & Pervin, L. A. (eds.) *Handbook of Personality* — reference, chapters as assigned.
49. `[R]` DeYoung, C. G., Quilty, L. C., & Peterson, J. B. (2007). *Between facets and domains: 10 aspects of the Big Five.* **JPSP**, 93(5), 880–896. The trait structure below the five.
50. `[R]` Roberts, B. W., et al. (2006). *Patterns of mean-level change in personality traits across the life course.* **Psychological Bulletin**, 132(1), 1–25. Traits change, systematically, with age — which undercuts most of what people assume about "personality."
51. `[R]` Mischel, W. *Personality and Assessment* (1968) — the book that broke trait psychology, then partly recovered from. The person-situation debate at the source.
52. `[R]` Plomin, R. *Blueprint* (2018) — behavioral genetics for a general reader. **Pair with a critique**; the book's confidence outruns the design's warrant in places.
53. `[R]` Turkheimer, E. (2000). *Three laws of behavior genetics and what they mean.* **Current Directions in Psychological Science**, 9(5), 160–164. Four pages. Read it twice.
54. `[R]` Bowlby, J. *A Secure Base* (1988), and Ainsworth's Strange Situation work — attachment at the source.
55. `[R]` Fraley, R. C., on attachment stability across the lifespan — the longitudinal evidence is weaker than the popular version. Find a recent review.
56. `[R]` McAdams, D. P. *The Redemptive Self* (2006), or his narrative-identity papers — the story a person tells about themselves, treated as data.
57. `[R]` van der Kolk, B. *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) — **read adversarially.** Enormously influential, clinically resonant, and its evidentiary claims run well ahead of the evidence in places. The exercise is separating the strong trauma research from the overreach, which is exactly the Week 43 skill.
58. `[R]` McNally, R. J. *Remembering Trauma* (2003) — the rigorous counterweight to #57. **Read them in the same fortnight.**
59. `[R]` Frances, A. *Saving Normal* (2013) — DSM classification and its expansion, by a former DSM task-force chair.
60. `[R]` Tavris, C. & Aronson, E. *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)* — self-justification. The last book of the year, deliberately: read it right before you write up a capstone whose framework probably underperformed.
